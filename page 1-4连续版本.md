# Research Strategy / Technical Approach

## 1. Technical Rationale, Opportunity, and Project Positioning

The DOE Genesis Mission identifies **AI for Scientific Reasoning** as a critical frontier for next-generation scientific discovery. Topic 19 states that, while current large language models (LLMs) are highly capable at processing and generating natural language, they remain limited on complex scientific tasks requiring deep analytical and mathematical reasoning, hypothesis generation from complex data, and rigorous, verifiable, multimodal reasoning. The FOA therefore calls for a new class of AI systems specifically architected for scientific reasoning, built around powerful base LLMs that can be integrated with other foundation models and enhanced through external scientific tools. It further highlights the importance of sophisticated AI agents that can formulate hypotheses, design tests, analyze results, and refine understanding in a closed-loop discovery engine. Within this topic, Focus Area **19C – Composable and Modular Foundation Models** specifically seeks **strong base LLMs designed for flexible integration with other specialized foundation models and/or tools to enable complex, cross-domain reasoning**.

This proposal is designed to respond directly to that language. We propose **SOVEREIGN-VIROFM**, a **sovereign, multi-agent, composable scientific foundation model system** for viral protein reasoning. The project is motivated by a central scientific and technical gap: modern AI for biology is highly fragmented. General LLMs can summarize or discuss biology fluently, but they do not reliably perform mechanistic, tool-grounded scientific reasoning. Specialized biological models may perform well on narrow tasks, but they are usually isolated from one another and difficult to combine into broader discovery workflows. As a result, existing systems are weak at integrating sequence, structure, evolution, statistics, and prior evidence into coherent, auditable scientific judgments. The proposed project addresses this gap by treating **composability** as the primary architectural principle rather than as an after-the-fact integration exercise. That positioning is what makes the project responsive to 19C rather than merely a domain-specific bioinformatics application.

Viral protein science provides an especially strong testbed for this effort. Reasoning about viral emergence, host adaptation, and immune escape is inherently cross-domain. Meaningful conclusions require coordinated analysis across amino-acid sequence, biomolecular structure, phylogenetic and evolutionary context, quantitative ranking and uncertainty, and prior mechanistic evidence from literature and curated resources. No single model can robustly cover all of these dimensions. We therefore frame the problem not as “training a virus model,” but as developing a **composable scientific reasoning architecture** in which a governed base scientific LLM coordinates multiple specialized models and tools, including a **from-scratch viral protein foundation model**, to support trustworthy viral biology workflows. This framing aligns with Topic 19’s emphasis on scientific reasoning and tool use, and with 19C’s emphasis on integrating strong base LLMs with specialized models for complex cross-domain reasoning.

A second defining feature of the project is its commitment to a **sovereign scientific model stack**. In this proposal, “sovereign” refers not merely to model ownership, but to a governed end-to-end computational pipeline in which training data, model weights, workflow traces, evaluation artifacts, and deployment pathways are auditable, reproducible, and suitable for trusted execution in U.S.-controlled scientific computing environments. This is not only a governance or infrastructure issue; it is a scientific requirement. A scientific AI system can only become a reliable contributor to discovery if its evidence sources, intermediate reasoning steps, confidence characteristics, and computational lineage can be inspected and reproduced. This emphasis on provenance, reproducibility, and trustworthy integration is consistent with the broader Genesis Mission philosophy of producing reusable AI models, workflows, and artifacts that may ultimately be integrated into larger national scientific AI ecosystems.

The central hypothesis of the project is that **a governed base scientific LLM, when coupled through a multi-agent orchestration layer to a domain-specialized viral protein foundation model and other scientific tools, will outperform non-composable baselines in viral scientific reasoning while providing stronger provenance, uncertainty awareness, and auditability**. The key contribution is therefore architectural as much as algorithmic. The aim is not to produce a virus-specific chatbot or a single-task biological predictor. The aim is to demonstrate a prototype of a new class of DOE-relevant scientific AI systems: **modular, agentic, tool-enabled foundation model architectures for cross-domain scientific reasoning**.

## 2. System Architecture and Agent Design

The proposed system is organized as a **multi-agent, provenance-aware scientific reasoning environment** in which a governed base scientific LLM coordinates specialized model agents and external tools through standardized interfaces. This design directly instantiates the FOA’s vision of powerful base LLMs integrated with specialized models and external scientific tools in a closed-loop scientific workflow. In technical terms, the system functions as a structured agent graph in which each agent contributes a distinct form of scientific evidence and returns outputs that can be inspected, reused, and composed across workflows. 

At the top of the architecture is the **Base Scientific LLM Orchestrator Agent**. This is the control layer of the system. Its role is to interpret a scientific question, decompose it into analyzable subtasks, determine which specialized agents or tools should be invoked, sequence or parallelize those invocations, integrate returned evidence, and produce a final reasoning trace and recommendation. Inputs to the orchestrator include the scientific question itself, structured task context, prior workflow state when available, and the outputs of previously called agents. Its outputs include a task plan, an execution trace, a synthesized reasoning summary, a ranked set of interpretations or hypotheses, and an uncertainty-aware final response. It also produces machine-readable records of provenance to support evaluation and audit. In Phase I, this component will be adapted rather than pretrained from scratch. The adaptation target is not generic conversational fluency, but reliable scientific planning, tool-aware reasoning, and evidence synthesis. Training data will therefore emphasize scientific task decomposition, tool routing, structured evidence interpretation, and auditable workflow behavior. This directly supports 19C’s emphasis on **strong base LLMs designed for flexible integration**.

The principal specialized model in the system is the **Viral Protein Foundation Agent**. This is the main differentiating module of the proposal and the component most directly tied to the user’s original scientific idea. Its purpose is to provide domain-specific representation learning for viral proteins, capturing sequence grammar, mutation context, functional constraints, and evolutionary regularities that may be only weakly represented in more general protein language models. Inputs include viral protein sequences, mutation lists, aligned or unaligned sequence sets, and optional contextual information such as subtype, clade, or region annotations. Outputs include sequence embeddings, residue-level saliency or importance maps, mutation-effect priors, similarity measures, and structured summaries of sequence-context signals relevant to downstream reasoning. This module will be developed as a **from-scratch prototype viral protein foundation model** trained on curated viral protein sequence corpora, using bounded Phase I scale appropriate to a nine-month proof of concept. Pretraining will focus on learning reusable viral sequence representations rather than optimizing a single downstream task. This is precisely how the module becomes responsive to 19C: it is a specialized foundation model designed from the outset to be integrated into a broader composable reasoning system rather than deployed in isolation. 

The **Structure Reasoning Agent** translates sequence-level observations into structural hypotheses. Its purpose is to assess how mutations or mutation combinations may alter local structure, receptor-binding geometry, epitope exposure, residue-neighborhood relationships, or other structure-linked properties relevant to antigenic escape and host adaptation. Inputs include sequences or variants, mutation sets, structural templates where available, and priors from the Viral Protein Foundation Agent. Outputs include structural compatibility assessments, residue-neighborhood summaries, mutation-impact interpretations, and ranked structural hypotheses with confidence annotations. In Phase I, this agent will be built primarily through adapter-based integration rather than full structural model pretraining. Existing structure-capable models and tools will be wrapped in standardized interfaces, and lightweight reasoning layers will translate raw structural outputs into machine-readable evidence objects suitable for multi-agent synthesis. This approach matches the FOA’s emphasis on flexible integration and workflow composition rather than de novo reinvention of every scientific component. 

The **Evolutionary Dynamics Agent** provides evolutionary plausibility and historical context. Its role is to determine whether observed mutations, sequence clusters, or candidate hypotheses are consistent with known evolutionary patterns, clade structure, conservation relationships, or plausible adaptive trajectories. Inputs include sequences, mutation sets, optional lineage or clade metadata, temporal or geographic labels where available, and candidate interpretations from the orchestrator or other agents. Outputs include phylogenetic plausibility scores, mutation-context histories, clade association summaries, and ranked adaptive hypotheses with uncertainty-aware interpretation. In Phase I, this agent will also rely primarily on tool-mediated integration and task-specific wrappers rather than full custom model development. The key innovation lies in bringing evolutionary evidence into the same reasoning space as sequence, structure, statistics, and literature evidence. This is strongly aligned with Topic 19’s stated interest in cross-domain reasoning across scientific evidence types. 

The **Quantitative Inference Agent** provides statistical analysis, calibration, ranking, and uncertainty estimation. This agent is essential because it prevents the system from becoming a purely narrative generator. Inputs include candidate mutation hypotheses, scores from other agents, benchmark labels when available, and workflow outputs that require calibration or prioritization. Outputs include calibrated rankings, uncertainty summaries, consistency checks, and evidence-quality flags, including explicit signals when evidence is weak or ambiguous. This agent will be implemented through tool-centric composition plus light reasoning adaptation, connecting robust statistical and evaluation packages to the broader multi-agent framework. Its role is especially important for Phase I because the Genesis Mission explicitly expects quantitative analysis of workflow performance and AI advantage. 

The **Evidence Agent** provides external scientific grounding through retrieval and reconciliation of relevant literature, curated databases, and domain knowledge resources. Inputs include scientific questions, mutation lists, candidate mechanisms proposed by other agents, and domain concepts requiring contextualization. Outputs include retrieved evidence summaries, support or contradiction statements, evidence-strength rankings, and provenance-linked citations, along with flags for sparse or conflicting evidence. This agent will be implemented using retrieval-augmented adaptation rather than large-scale independent pretraining. Because Topic 19 emphasizes AI systems that move beyond summarization toward scientifically grounded discovery, the Evidence Agent is critical for linking model-generated interpretations to prior knowledge and making the final workflow more trustworthy and auditable. 

Across all agents, four architectural principles will be enforced. First, every agent will expose a **well-defined input/output schema** so that the orchestrator can invoke it reliably and integrate its outputs. Second, every agent will operate through a **tool interface layer** that records model version, input context, parameters, provenance, and confidence metadata. Third, every agent will support **uncertainty-aware outputs**, including confidence scores, abstention flags, or ranked hypotheses. Fourth, every agent will be built for **replaceability**, so that upgraded models or tools can be inserted later without rewriting the system. These principles are essential for translating 19C’s composability requirement into a concrete computational architecture. 

## 3. Training, Integration, and Evaluation Plan

The Phase I effort is designed as a **bounded proof of concept**. The FOA states that Phase I should demonstrate a clear, tangible research workflow and quantitatively assess whether the proposed approach is on a trajectory toward transformative scientific capability. Accordingly, the purpose of the initial nine-month effort is not to build a maximally scaled universal scientific AI platform, but to construct and evaluate a prototype that captures the essential architectural properties of a 19C-style composable and modular foundation model system. 

Training will proceed along two coordinated but distinct tracks. The first is **adaptation of the base scientific LLM orchestration layer**. The second is **pretraining of the prototype viral protein foundation model**. These are intentionally separated because they serve different roles. The base LLM must become effective at scientific task decomposition, tool routing, and evidence synthesis. The viral protein foundation model must become effective at domain-specific sequence representation learning. For the base orchestrator, training data will emphasize curated examples of scientific task decomposition, tool-use traces, structured evidence synthesis, and domain-relevant workflow demonstrations. The goal is to produce a dependable orchestration model for **agentic scientific planning** rather than a general-purpose conversational assistant. For the viral protein foundation model, Phase I will use a bounded corpus and bounded model scale, with pretraining objectives centered on learning reusable viral sequence representations and mutation-context signals. This restrained design is deliberate: the FOA asks not for uncontrolled scale, but for proof of concept, validation, and a credible trajectory toward broader capability.

The remaining agents—Structure, Evolutionary, Quantitative, and Evidence—will be implemented primarily through **adapter-based integration and tool wrapping**. In Phase I, the scientific value of these agents lies in their contribution to the composable system and the structure of their outputs, rather than in creating entirely new standalone foundation models for every evidence type. Wrappers, reasoning adapters, and translation layers will convert raw outputs from tools or existing models into machine-readable evidence objects that can be integrated by the orchestrator. This is both technically efficient and fully consistent with 19C, which focuses on flexible integration across specialized models and tools. 

Integration will proceed in stages. First, each agent will be developed and validated independently using shared schemas for inputs, outputs, provenance, and confidence metadata. Second, all agents will be connected to the orchestrator through a common invocation protocol that supports structured agent calls and structured evidence returns. Third, the system will be assembled into full end-to-end workflows for the two Phase I demonstration tasks: **antigenic escape assessment** and **cross-species adaptation analysis**. A central integration requirement is that every agent invocation produce a machine-readable workflow record containing task identifiers, model or tool versions, input and output summaries, parameter settings, timestamps, and confidence metadata. These workflow traces are essential both for debugging and for formal evaluation, and they embody the project’s commitment to a sovereign, auditable scientific model stack. 

Two demonstration workflows will anchor evaluation. In **antigenic escape assessment**, the system begins from a sequence variant or mutation set and determines whether the available evidence supports concern for altered antigenic properties. The orchestrator will use the Viral Protein Foundation Agent to obtain mutation-context priors, the Structure Reasoning Agent to assess possible epitope or receptor-proximal implications, the Evolutionary Dynamics Agent to assess plausibility and historical context, the Evidence Agent to retrieve supporting or contradictory scientific evidence, and the Quantitative Inference Agent to calibrate and rank the final interpretations. In **cross-species adaptation analysis**, the system will analyze whether a sequence, mutation profile, or candidate variant is consistent with host-adaptive change, with greater emphasis on mutation plausibility, historical host-associated patterns, and evidence reconciliation. These workflows directly implement Topic 19’s idea of an AI system that integrates multiple models and tools in a closed-loop scientific reasoning process. 

Evaluation is a central part of the technical plan. The FOA explicitly states that Phase I success may include increased predictive power, stronger scientific insight, tighter coupling of data and hypotheses, identification of scaling metrics, improved workflows, or other proposed measures demonstrating AI advantage. Our evaluation framework will therefore operate at three levels: **component performance**, **workflow performance**, and **AI advantage trajectory**. At the component level, we will assess whether individual agents are functioning as intended—for example, whether the viral protein foundation model produces usable domain-specific signals and whether the orchestrator reliably performs task decomposition and tool routing. At the workflow level, we will compare the full multi-agent system against three baseline categories: a single-model baseline, a non-agentic compositional baseline in which tools are manually chained, and a manually assembled workflow using the same tools outside the agent framework. Metrics will include predictive utility, reasoning coherence, evidence traceability, uncertainty calibration, and time-to-hypothesis. At the trajectory level, we will assess whether performance improves as additional evidence sources or agent capabilities are added—for example, whether moving from sequence-only to sequence-plus-structure reasoning measurably improves system behavior. This last point is especially important because the FOA asks whether a system is on a **credible trajectory toward transformative scientific capability**, not merely whether a fixed prototype performs one task well.

## 4. Milestones, Timeline, and Go/No-Go Criteria

The Phase I effort is organized as a **nine-month, milestone-driven proof of concept**. This timeline is designed to satisfy the Genesis Mission expectation that teams define a clear workflow, validate their approach quantitatively, and determine whether the results justify larger Phase II investment. The milestones are therefore structured around three objectives: demonstrating the technical feasibility of the multi-agent architecture, validating measurable AI advantage on representative viral science workflows, and producing a credible scaling roadmap for Phase II. 

In **Months 1–2**, the project will finalize the agent communication protocol, define shared schemas for evidence objects and provenance, establish versioning and uncertainty-tracking standards, assemble the curated viral protein corpus, and stand up the baseline orchestration environment. The goal of this stage is to ensure that the project has a governed data and artifact pipeline plus the minimum infrastructure required for model adaptation and independent agent testing.

In **Months 3–4**, the project will adapt the base scientific LLM for scientific task decomposition, tool routing, and evidence synthesis while also training the prototype viral protein foundation model. By the end of this stage, the orchestrator should be able to invoke stubbed or early-stage agents using the shared protocol, and the viral module should produce usable domain-specialized outputs for downstream workflows.

In **Months 5–6**, the project will integrate the Structure, Evolutionary, Quantitative, and Evidence agents using tool wrappers and translation layers, and connect all agents to the orchestrator through the common protocol. By the end of this stage, the system should be capable of executing multi-step workflows with complete provenance logging and structured confidence propagation.

In **Months 7–8**, the project will run the two full demonstration workflows and compare the assembled multi-agent system to predefined baselines. Evaluation will generate quantitative evidence on predictive utility, reasoning coherence, evidence traceability, uncertainty calibration, and workflow efficiency, and will also support error analysis and workflow refinement.

In **Month 9**, the project will consolidate results, finalize the evaluation package, organize workflow artifacts and documentation, and complete a Phase II readiness assessment. This final stage will produce not only a working prototype and evaluation report, but also a formal recommendation on whether the architecture justifies scaling to a larger follow-on effort. 

The project will use explicit **go/no-go criteria** to guide this decision. First, the base scientific LLM must reliably execute structured task decomposition and agent routing without brittle protocol failure. Second, the viral protein foundation model must provide domain-specific outputs that are actually useful within the integrated workflow rather than merely duplicating generic sequence features. Third, all five agents must operate within a shared provenance-aware framework that preserves structured outputs, confidence information, and reproducibility. Fourth, the system must demonstrate measurable value relative to less integrated baselines in at least some of the targeted AI advantage metrics, such as reasoning coherence, evidence traceability, workflow efficiency, uncertainty calibration, or predictive utility. Fifth, the project must produce a technically coherent rationale for Phase II scaling in model capacity, tool breadth, scientific scope, and computing integration. These criteria reflect the FOA’s broad view of Phase I success, which may be established through improved prediction, stronger scientific insight, better workflow performance, or evidence that additional data, compute, or model integration would yield further gains. 

## 5. Expected Outcome and Strategic Impact

At the conclusion of Phase I, the expected outcome is a **well-documented prototype of a sovereign, multi-agent, composable scientific foundation model system** that has been tested on two scientifically meaningful viral reasoning workflows and evaluated quantitatively against less integrated alternatives. Even if every component is not yet fully optimized, success will be defined by whether the project establishes a defensible case that this architecture can evolve into a broader scientific reasoning capability under Phase II support. In that sense, the project is not simply a model-building exercise. It is a test of the central 19C hypothesis that strong base LLMs, when flexibly integrated with specialized foundation models and scientific tools, can create new forms of **cross-domain AI advantage for science**.

Beyond viral biology, the resulting architecture is intended to serve as a reusable template for DOE-relevant scientific AI systems that require auditable, modular, tool-enabled reasoning across heterogeneous computational components. The broader significance is therefore twofold. Scientifically, the project creates a new way to reason about viral evolution, host adaptation, and immune escape by integrating multiple evidence streams into an active discovery workflow. Programmatically, it demonstrates how a strong base scientific LLM can be combined with domain-specialized foundation models and external tools in a governed multi-agent environment that is aligned with the Genesis Mission’s long-term platform vision. That combination of domain depth, architectural modularity, and workflow-centered evaluation is what makes the project a strong conceptual fit for **Topic 19C – Composable and Modular Foundation Models**.
